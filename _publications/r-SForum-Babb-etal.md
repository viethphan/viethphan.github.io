***Abstract***: Sociologists of law argue that law inside organizations is distorted by the agendas of organizational managers to minimize its impact (Edelman et al. 1999; Edelman et al. 2001). We complicate this theory by arguing that managers may promote two different conceptions of compliance—collective interpretations and policy scripts that define organizations’ responses to law. One is the good faith conception, which emphasizes minimizing the impact of law. The other is the internal controls conception, which emphasizes minimizing compliance risk. How do American compliance workers put these conceptions of compliance into practice in their everyday work activities?  We address these questions in a content analysis of an original dataset of 994 American compliance job listings. We find that American compliance work is structured at once by discretionary interpretation, rulemaking, and training (linked to the good faith conception); and accountability duties (associated with the internal controls conception). These findings have implications for how law may succeed or fail to achieve its objectives in organizations today.
